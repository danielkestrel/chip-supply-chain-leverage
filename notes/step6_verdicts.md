# Step 6: credibility, and the final sort

## Credibility rule (set before judging any ask)

Credibility asks whether the coalition could actually get the ask. It is scored low, medium or high:

- **high:** the US would plausibly accept on its own terms, because the ask costs it little or helps it. No leverage is needed.
- **medium:** the US government or US firms would resist, but the ask can be backed by a **condition** on supply rather than a cut. It also lines up with at least part of US policy (Congress, agencies or firms), so the coalition would be pushing with an existing US current, not against it.
- **low:** it needs a **threat to cut supply** or collective supply management, or it would be read in Washington as coercion against US policy. Step 3 found that threats failed or backfired whenever the target could replace supply within about 5 years, and Step 4 found that this describes 27 of today's 35 chokepoint rows.

**Sorting rule (from the plan):** "coordinate on" means credible (medium or high) **and** worth having (medium or high safety value). Everything else is "avoid".

## Asks with no threat behind them: would the US accept them anyway?

- **A1 · Anti-diversion and end-use checks: high credibility.** Stopping advanced chips from reaching unauthorised end users is the stated purpose of US export controls. Since 2023 the US has pressed the Netherlands and Japan to align their own controls. Shared checks cost the US little and extend its reach, so the coalition would be offering something Washington already asks allies for.

## Asks that need leverage: does the profile look like a case that worked or one that backfired?

Here is what Steps 3 and 4 say:
- **Threats only held where replacement took more than 5 years** (the oil embargo, which only partly worked).
- **27 of 35 chokepoint rows can be replaced within about 5 years.** Most of today's profile is closest to Japan–Korea 2019 (21 rows) and CIPEC (12 rows), which backfired or failed.
- **The 8 rows with long replacement times** (ASML lithography, Taiwan's leading-edge fabs, Tokyo Electron's resist processing, Canon's i-line tools, e-beam mask writers) **are as concentrated as China–Japan 2010 and Japan–Korea 2019, which both backfired.**
- **Nearly every holder depends on the US:** 32 of 35 rows score high on dependence on the US for security. So a group threat would face strong US pressure on each member, much as the oil embargo leaked through companies redirecting cargoes.

The verdicts:

- **A2 · Frontier model access for members' evaluators: medium credibility.**
  - **For:** US labs already give pre-deployment access to the US and UK institutes voluntarily. Members can ask for the same as a condition of deeper cooperation (for example, alongside A1), without any cut.
  - **Against:** the reported 2026 White House move to sequence UK access after US checks shows the US government wants control of timing. That argues for a negotiated slot, not open access.
- **A3 · Safety conditions on large compute buyers: low credibility.**
  - It binds US labs and cloud firms through conditions set abroad, which Washington would likely read as reaching beyond the coalition's own jurisdiction.
  - The coalition's chokepoints sit several contract steps upstream of the buyers (ASML → TSMC → Nvidia → clouds → labs), so enforcing it would in practice mean threatening to withhold tools or fab capacity.
  - That is the threat pattern that backfired in Step 3. The US is already building replacement fab capacity (TSMC Arizona, Intel), so using the threat would speed that up.
- **A4 · Support for hardware verification features: medium credibility.**
  - **For:** the US Congress is moving the same way (the Chip Security Act, introduced 2025, not enacted as of May 2026).
  - **Against:** Nvidia opposes it, so the coalition would be backing one part of US policy against a US firm.
  - Coalition firms that make or package the chips (TSMC, SK hynix) could support common standards **without** a cut. Credible as support for a US-led requirement, not as a demand on its own.
- **A5 · Withholding supply: low credibility.** It is a threat to cut, against a target that can replace most of the supply within 5 years. Members rely on that target for their security, and buyers organise after every cut (as the oil embargo showed). It is likely to backfire.
- **A6 · Coordinating prices: low credibility.** Both price-management cases (CIPEC, the tin council) failed or backfired, and it would breach EU, Japanese and Korean competition law.
- **A7 · Threatening to sell to China: low credibility.**
  - The Netherlands and Japan have aligned their export controls with the US since 2023.
  - The threat would contradict A1.
  - Members rely on the US for their security, so the threat would not be believed, and carrying it out would invite US retaliation.
  - Its medium safety value (Andrew's judgment) does not rescue it, because the problem is credibility.

## The sort

| Coordinate on (credible and worth having) | Avoid (not credible, or likely to backfire) |
| --- | --- |
| **A1** Shared anti-diversion and end-use checks | **A3** Binding safety conditions on large compute buyers, as stated |
| **A2** Model access for members' evaluators, negotiated alongside A1 | **A5** Withholding supply |
| **A4** Support for US-led hardware verification standards | **A6** Coordinating prices |
| | **A7** Threatening to sell to China |

- **The hypothesis holds.** Every credible ask is either something the US already wants or a condition that works with part of US policy. Every threat or supply-management move lands in "avoid".
- **The tension worth stating:** the ask with the most direct safety value (A3) is not credible as a binding condition. A weaker version could fold into A1, for example know-your-customer checks on very large compute buyers, which the US also pursues. This is a suggestion, not something the project tested.

## Which verdicts would flip if a disputed score went the other way

| Disputed score | Other reading | Verdicts that flip |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **K5 outcome** (Japan–Korea): backfired | Partly worked, since Korea offered its own compensation plan in 2023 | The rule "threats only held where replacement took more than 5 years" would weaken, and 21 of today's rows would most resemble a case that partly worked. **A3 could move to medium credibility and flip to "coordinate on".** A5 and A6 stay "avoid" (low safety value), and A7 stays "avoid", because its low credibility rests on alliance dependence, not on the cases. |
| **Time to replace for the strongest rows** (ASML lithography, Taiwan's fabs): high | Medium, if US fabs and alternatives arrive faster | **None.** No row would then resemble the oil embargo, which makes threat-backed asks even less credible. |
| **A2 needs leverage**: yes (uncertain) | No, if the US government doesn't actually restrict allied testing | **None.** A2 rises to high credibility but is already "coordinate on". |
| **A7 safety value**: medium (Andrew) | Low (first draft) | **None.** A7 is "avoid" on credibility either way. |

**The verdict that most depends on a judgment is A3.** Whether binding buyer conditions are credible turns on how one reads Japan–Korea 2019, and on whether conditions passed down several contracts could be enforced without a threat.
