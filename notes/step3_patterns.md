# Step 3 patterns: what the past cases show

Built from the final case scores in `scoring/cases_final.csv`. The case details are in `notes/step3_cases.md`.

## Scores against outcomes

| Case | Conc. | Subst. | Time to replace | Cohesion | Dependence | State control | Outcome |
| --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- | --- |
| K1 Oil embargo 1973–74 | medium | medium | **high** | low | high | high | partly worked |
| K2 CIPEC 1967– | medium | **high** | medium | medium | high | medium | failed |
| K3 Tin council 1985 | medium | **high** | medium | medium | medium | medium | backfired |
| K4 China–Japan 2010 | high | low | medium | low | high | high | backfired |
| K5 Japan–Korea 2019–23 | high | medium | medium | low | high | medium | backfired |

Direction: low substitutability helps the supplier, and so does low dependence on the target. For the other four variables, high helps the supplier.

## Rules of thumb

1. **Leverage only held where the target needed more than 5 years to replace supply.** The oil embargo (K1) is the only case that partly worked, and the only one scored high on time to replace. In all four cases where the target could replace supply within about 5 years (K2–K5), the leverage failed or backfired.
2. **Supply management fails outright when substitutes are easy.** Both cases with high substitutability (K2 copper, K3 tin) never gained, or quickly lost, any hold over the market. The tin case also shows that propping up prices draws in new supply and substitutes, which leaves the group worse off.
3. **A near-monopoly is not enough if the target can diversify within a few years.** China in 2010 (K4) and Japan in 2019 (K5) both held a high share of what the target needed, and both backfired. The target built or bought alternatives (Australian rare earths; Korean hydrogen fluoride), so the leverage was spent in one use.
4. **Even a cut that works makes buyers organise.** The oil embargo led directly to the International Energy Agency, stockpiles and new non-Arab supply, which wore the leverage down over the following decade. Expect any visible threat to speed up the target's replacement.

## Variables that don't separate successes from failures

- **Dependence on the target:** high in the case that partly worked (K1) and in three that failed or backfired (K2, K4, K5).
- **State control:** high in K1 (partly worked) and K4 (backfired), and medium in the rest.
- **Cohesion (counted):** low in K1 (partly worked) and in K4 and K5 (backfired), and medium in the two failures. If anything it runs the "wrong" way, so the actor count may not capture what holds a group together.
- **Concentration:** the two highest scores (K4, K5) both backfired. On its own it doesn't predict success.
- **Substitutability:** it separates the two outright failures from the rest, but not the one success from the backfires.
- **What does separate them:** only **time to replace**.
- **Caveat:** with five cases these are suggestions, not tests. A single changed score can flip a pattern.

## Factors the six variables may be missing

- **The kind of demand.** A specific political ask with a face-saving path (K1: disengagement talks) differs from a demand the target can't meet without losing face (K4, K5), and from a price target (K2, K3).
- **Whether the supplier gains even without a concession.** Oil producers earned far more from higher prices whatever the US did. The tin council and CIPEC had no such cushion.
- **Market conditions at the time.** The 1973 oil market was tight. The copper and tin markets of the mid-1970s and 1980s were slack.
- **Stockpiles and money.** US stockpiles (tin) undercut the supplier, and the tin council collapsed when its borrowed money ran out. Neither is one of the six variables.
- **Leakage through intermediaries.** Oil companies redirected cargoes, which spread the embargo's impact across all buyers.
- **Uncertainty over intent.** In K4 it is disputed whether China deliberately targeted Japan, and in K5 Japan officially cited export-control concerns, not the court rulings. Ambiguous coercion may have different effects from openly declared coercion.
